## 2. Bài đọc

> **The bicycle: two centuries of ups and downs**
>
> **A.** Few inventions have been declared outdated and then rediscovered as often as the bicycle. Today it is promoted as a cheap, healthy and clean answer to urban congestion, yet the machine is more than two hundred years old. Its history is not a simple story of steady progress; rather, it is a cycle of enthusiasm, decline and revival, shaped as much by social attitudes and public policy as by engineering.
>
> **B.** The earliest ancestor of the bicycle appeared in 1817, when the German inventor Karl Drais demonstrated his 'running machine' in Mannheim. It had two wheels in line and a front wheel that could be steered, but no pedals: riders sat on a wooden frame and pushed themselves along with their feet. Some historians suggest that Drais was looking for an alternative to the horse after the disastrous harvest of 1816, when many animals died for lack of feed, although there is little direct evidence for this. The device attracted brief public curiosity before the fashion faded.
>
> **C.** In the 1860s, French makers attached pedals directly to the front wheel, producing a machine known in English as the 'boneshaker' because of its iron tyres and the rough roads of the time. To travel faster, designers simply enlarged the front wheel, and by the 1870s the high-wheeled 'penny-farthing' had become the symbol of cycling. It was quick, but mounting it required skill, and a sudden stop could throw the rider over the handlebars. Unsurprisingly, it was ridden mainly by young men with money and a taste for risk.
>
> **D.** The decisive change came in 1885, when John Kemp Starley produced the Rover, a 'safety bicycle' with two wheels of similar size and a chain driving the rear wheel. Three years later, John Boyd Dunlop, a Scottish vet working in Belfast, developed an air-filled tyre to make his young son's tricycle more comfortable; the idea was quickly adopted by bicycle manufacturers. With a lower, more stable design and a smoother ride, cycling became accessible to people who would never have attempted a penny-farthing. Mass production lowered prices, and the 1890s saw a cycling boom in Europe and North America. Women in particular gained a new independence: the American campaigner Susan B. Anthony remarked in 1896 that the bicycle had done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world.
>
> **E.** The twentieth century was less kind. As cars became affordable, many cities were redesigned around the motor car, and in much of Europe and North America cycling came to be seen as something for children or for those who could not afford anything better. In the Netherlands, however, rising numbers of road deaths, many of them children, led to public protests in the early 1970s, and the oil crisis of 1973 prompted the government to introduce car-free Sundays. These events helped to persuade Dutch planners to invest in separate cycle paths, a policy that Denmark also pursued.
>
> **F.** Recent decades have brought a revival. One of the earliest attempts to share bicycles took place in Amsterdam in 1965, when activists painted bicycles white and left them unlocked for anyone to use; within a short time, most had been stolen or removed by the police. Later schemes learned from this failure by using docking stations and payment cards, and the launch of Vélib' in Paris in 2007 showed that bike-sharing could work on a large scale. More recently, electric bicycles have extended the distances people are willing to ride and made hilly cities far more manageable, particularly for older riders.
>
> **G.** Yet technology alone does not explain why cycling flourishes in some places and not in others. Where cycle lanes are merely painted onto busy roads, most people remain unwilling to ride; where they are physically separated from traffic, numbers tend to rise. In my view, the bicycle's future therefore depends less on new inventions than on the willingness of governments to give it space. The history of the last two centuries suggests that the machine itself has rarely been the problem.

## 4. Đáp án và giải thích

> [!tip]- Đáp án Questions 1–5 (Matching Headings)
> 1. **B – iii**: *two wheels... but no pedals: riders... pushed themselves along with their feet*.
> 2. **C – i**: đoạn kết bằng câu chốt *ridden mainly by young men with money*. *viii* là bẫy chi tiết: *rough roads* chỉ giải thích tên gọi *boneshaker*.
> 3. **D – vi**: xe an toàn, lốp hơi, giá giảm → *accessible to people who would never have attempted a penny-farthing*.
> 4. **E – v**: ô tô đẩy xe đạp ra lề (*pushed aside*), rồi Hà Lan và Đan Mạch đầu tư đường xe đạp (*defended*). *iv* là bẫy trùng từ *motor car*: đoạn không nói vì sao người đi xe đạp chuyển sang ô tô.
> 5. **F – ii**: thí nghiệm năm 1965 thất bại → hệ thống trạm, Vélib' thành công, xe đạp điện.
> - Heading thừa: *iv, vii, viii*. *vii* không được nhắc.

> [!tip]- Đáp án Questions 6–10 (True / False / Not Given)
> 6. **FALSE**: *no pedals*. Bàn đạp gắn bánh trước là kiểu xe những năm 1860 (đoạn C), bẫy trộn thông tin giữa hai đoạn.
> 7. **TRUE**: *ridden mainly by young men with money*.
> 8. **FALSE**: *to make his young son's tricycle more comfortable*.
> 9. **NOT GIVEN**: bài chỉ nói *mass production lowered prices*, không so với giá ngựa. Ngựa chỉ được nhắc ở đoạn B, năm 1816.
> 10. **FALSE**: *within a short time, most had been stolen or removed by the police*.

> [!tip]- Đáp án Questions 11–13 (Summary completion)
> 11. **motor car** (*redesigned around the motor car*; ghi *car* cũng đúng nghĩa và trong giới hạn từ, nhưng chép đủ cụm trong bài là an toàn nhất).
> 12. **oil crisis** (bản tóm tắt đã có *of 1973*, nên không ghi lại năm).
> 13. **electric bicycles** (*have extended the distances people are willing to ride and made hilly cities far more manageable*).
